Introduction

Climatic Conditions in Burkina Faso, Togo, and Côte d’Ivoire

Burkina Faso, Togo, and Côte d’Ivoire are all located in West Africa and are influenced by the dynamics of the West African Monsoon system. This system drives seasonal rainfall, with precipitation levels generally increasing from the arid Sahelian north to the humid coastal south. Burkina Faso, located further inland, has a semi-arid to tropical climate, characterized by a long dry season and a short rainy season from June to September. In contrast, Togo and Côte d’Ivoire experience more humid, tropical climates, especially in their southern regions, with two rainy seasons (typically from March to July and September to November) and higher annual rainfall.

Temperatures across all three countries remain high year-round, often exceeding 35°C during the peak of the dry season, particularly in northern Burkina Faso. Coastal regions, particularly in Côte d’Ivoire, experience slightly lower temperatures and higher humidity due to the influence of the Atlantic Ocean. Useful Articles / Studies

In recent decades, all three countries have faced increasing climate variability and extreme weather events, such as prolonged droughts in Burkina Faso (notably during the 1970s and 1980s), and urban and rural flooding in Togo and Côte d’Ivoire, exacerbated by intense rainfall and poor drainage infrastructure. These trends are consistent with broader regional patterns linked to climate change, including shifts in monsoon onset, erratic rainfall distribution, and more frequent extreme heat events.

# Cote d’Ivoire (https://bluegreenatlas.com/climate/cote_divoire_climate.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com) Côte d’Ivoire has a warm, humid climate that transitions from equatorial to tropical. Temperatures average between 25°C (75°F) and 32°C (90°F), with extremes of 10°C (50°F) to 40°C (104°F) depending on the time of year and the area of the country.

In the north, heavy rains occur between June and October, averaging 1100 millimeters (43 inches) annually. Along the coast and the southwest, some rain falls in most months, but rain is heaviest between May and September, with average rainfall of 1100 to 2000 millimeters (43 to 87 inches) annually. The major dry season lasts from December to April.

#Burkina Faso https://bluegreenatlas.com/climate/burkina_faso_climate.html The climate is one of high temperatures, especially at the end of the dry season. The harmattan, a dry east wind, brings with it spells of considerable heat from March to May; from May to October, the climate is hot and wet, and from November to March, comfortable and dry.

Average annual rainfall varies from 1,150 mm (45 in) in the southwest to less than 250 mm (10 in) in the extreme north and northeast. The rainy season lasts from four months to six months, from May through October. Droughts are common.

# Togo https://bluegreenatlas.com/climate/togo_climate.html Togo has a humid, tropical climate. Temperatures average around 27°C (81°F) across most of the country, but with much greater variation in the north than the south.

In the south there are two rainy seasons, from April to July and in September and October. The coast gets the least rainfall, about 800 mm (32 in) annually. The heaviest rainfall occurs in the hills of the west, southwest, and center, where the rainfall averages about 1500 mm (60 in) a year. North of the Togo Mountains there is one rainy season, lasting from May to September. Rainfall in this region averages 1000 mm (40 in) a year.

Sea-Level Rise and Coastal Erosion in Togo and Côte d’Ivoire Togo and Côte d’Ivoire, both situated along the Gulf of Guinea in West Africa, are increasingly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change, particularly sea-level rise (SLR) and coastal erosion. These low-lying coastal zones are home to major cities such as Lomé and Abidjan, and support significant economic activities, including ports, tourism, and agriculture.

According to the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report, sea level along the West African coast has risen by approximately 3.5 mm per year over the past three decades, a rate consistent with the global average but with higher exposure risks due to the concentration of population and infrastructure in vulnerable coastal zones (IPCC, 2021). The West African coastline is also subject to high natural variability, but human activities — such as sand mining, dam construction upstream, and poorly planned urban development — have intensified erosion and reduced sediment supply to beaches.

Côte d’Ivoire is experiencing shoreline retreat in several areas, with average coastal erosion rates estimated at 1 to 2 meters per year, though some hotspots, such as Grand-Lahou, have reported higher rates (World Bank, 2019). Similarly, Togo’s coastline is severely eroding, particularly east of Lomé, where retreat rates of up to 5–10 meters per year have been observed. In both countries, port construction and shoreline hardening have altered natural sediment dynamics, leading to downstream erosion.

Rising sea levels are expected to increase the frequency and intensity of coastal flooding, storm surges, and saline intrusion. Projections suggest that, under high emissions scenarios (RCP8.5), relative sea level along the West African coast could rise by 0.75 to 1 meter by 2100, dramatically increasing the area and population exposed to flooding and erosion (IPCC, 2021). This presents significant challenges for coastal resilience, especially in informal settlements and ecologically sensitive areas like lagoons and estuaries.

The World Bank estimates that coastal degradation — including erosion, flooding, and pollution — is costing Togo, Côte d’Ivoire, and other West African coastal states over $3.8 billion annually, or 5.3% of Côte d’Ivoire’s GDP and 2.3% of Togo’s GDP (World Bank, 2019).
